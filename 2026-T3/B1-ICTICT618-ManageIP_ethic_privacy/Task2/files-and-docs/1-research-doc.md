**Intellectual property and copyright standard/legislation**

The main legislation for copyright is the Copyright Act 1968. Its primary purpose is to protect original creative works. It legally classifies the source code of software programs, automation scripts, and databases as "literary works". This legislation prevents the unauthorised use, copying, distribution, or reverse engineering of proprietary systems.

ICT industry standard for intellectual property: Who governs IP is the Australian Computer Society (ACS), the main standard is the Code of Professional Conduct, specifically under the values of "Honesty" and "Professionalism". The standard dictates that ICT professionals must respect proprietary rights and intellectual property. It requires staff to ensure that all software, diagnostic tools, and scripts used for network analysis or server maintenance are properly licensed. This means strictly adhering to corporate software whitelisting policies and avoiding the use of pirated code.

**Privacy legislation**

The primary legislation relevant to privacy in Australia is the Privacy Act 1988. Its key purpose is to regulate how personal information is collected, used, stored, and disclosed by organisations and government agencies. It establishes the 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs), which provide a strict framework for data handling. In an IT support environment, this act dictates the legal obligation to ensure that sensitive user data residing in databases, network logs, or enterprise applications is securely protected against unauthorised access at all times.

**Ethics**

An important standard in the industry is the Information Technology Professionals Association (ITPA) Code of Ethics, alongside the Australian Computer Society (ACS) Code of Ethics. The key purpose of these codes is to establish a mandatory framework of professional conduct, ensuring that IT staff act with honesty, integrity, and competence.

Typical review procedures involve regular audits of the company's code of conduct and periodic assessments to ensure the policies remain relevant to current technology trends. Grievance procedures for ethical breaches usually follow a structured, confidential reporting mechanism. If a staff member witnesses an ethical violation, such as a colleague gaining unauthorised access to a sensitive database via a terminal, they can report it confidentially to a compliance officer or management. The procedure then dictates an impartial fact-finding investigation, an opportunity for the accused to respond, and appropriate disciplinary actions if the breach is confirmed.
