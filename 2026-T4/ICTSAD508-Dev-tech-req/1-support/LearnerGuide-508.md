## Organisational requirements

Understanding an organisation’s requirements, standards, policies and procedures can provide information that can help you when developing technical requirements to enable business solutions to be implemented in an organisation.
Organisational requirements can include identifying an organisation’s vision, goals, objectives, priorities, business plans, systems and processes, legal requirements (as discussed previously), standards and security strategy.
In projects such as these, you would need to spend time finding out the organisation’s requirements, either through interviewing, discussing in face-to-face meetings, via email or by a client completing a fact finding document.
Specific organisational policies and procedures and standards can include:
- Workplace health and safety policies
- Information security policies
- Cyber security policies
- Documentation to be used for hardware and software solutions
- Procedures for planning and implementing projects such as following software development lifecycle methodologies
- Risk management procedures
- Using frameworks and methodologies.

Standards could include consumer standards, service level agreements, Australian standards for cyber security, web accessibility standards and following the Australian Computer Society Code of Ethics or following a structured methodology for system development such as the Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC).

A requirements report can then be written outlining the business requirements.  A technical requirements report would outline the technical requirements.

When undertaking the development of technical requirements for a business:
- focus on the client’s requirements
- select the technology needed to best support these needs.
Requirements can range from the straightforward (development of a business intranet) to the most complex (an e-commerce solution for selling cryptocurrency).

## Business models and solutions

Another important factor in establishing business solution requirements is to determine the business model.
Business models describes how an organisation creates, delivers, and captures value. It describes how the business operates.
Some of the common features of business models include the following:
"
Value proposition: a statement of what goods or services the company offers that are of value to potential customers or clients. As far as possible, this is done in a way that differentiates the what the company from what its competitors are.
Projected start-up or establishment costs and sources (and costs) of financing. These should be balanced against the projected revenues and expenses to establish the business’ viability.
Projections of revenues and expenses: establishing how much profit the business expects to make.
The target customer base for the goods or services the company offers.
The company’s marketing strategy: this will set out how the target customers will be contacted and the messages that they will receive.
"

## Business process design

Business process design is concerned with developing an effective approach to tasks that have to be performed within a business. Therefore, tasks are defined and then documented in a work flow that ensures they are performed to requirements and that no steps are missed. 

## Customer and business liaison processes 

Customer and business liaison processes provide integrated support services to customers. These business liaisons may focus on helping customers understand, explore and acquisition technology solutions. 
This may be through conducting consultation, interviews, meetings, seeking feedback or developing a communications strategy that outlines:
- the most appropriate method, platform and mode for communication
- how communication will take place
- how often it will take place
- roles and responsibilities
- contacts.

## Technical specifications
Technical specifications will outline both what the current ICT infrastructure is in place and also what is required.  
It can include integration, interface requirements, security protocols and also documentation such as data flow diagrams, ICT infrastructure diagrams, system specifications or detailed specifications of hardware and software.

## Documenting the proposed business solution

A requirements report is generally undertaken to confirm what the client has told you has been recorded and documented. This will generally include:
- Background of organisation or business (structure, technology in place)
- Problem definition (why are the options being reviewed)
- Issues or factors impinging on problem definition (for example, budget)
- What are the objectives (for example to upgrade old equipment)
- Criteria for achievement of objectives ( for example, coping with future demands)
- Resources available (what is currently being used)
- Possible strategies (options to be considered)
- Technology requirements (hardware software and networking)
- Plans for the future (for example expansion)
- Budget (how much money is available)
- Project specifications (data usage, backup, warrantee, support, external cloud storage)
- Timelines and schedules.

Once the proposed solution has been documented this can then be distributed for feedback and approval so that the project can move to the next stage.  

Approval may be in the form of a written contract, the requirements report signed-off, an email from a client or project management documentation signed off by various stakeholders such as a project manager.

## Identifying processes requiring change
When a software solution has been defined, you will then need to identify processes requiring change by the business solution. This can be carried out by:
- studying the inputs and outputs of data
- studying formal processes currently in place
- analysing and documenting data processes
- using tools to map processes such as flow charts or data flow diagrams
- analysing the processes, using tools such as Root Cause analysis or Cause and Affect analysis.

It is important to study the business processes of an organisation when determining the technical requirements and solutions for a client. This would include both hardware and software processes. For example, the way in which a customer can purchase from a website may change from using a guest login to requiring a user login. Consider changing from a business model where all staff are using a word processing package that has been installed on their computer to using one that is via a cloud service. You can imagine how many processes would need to be changed in order for this to be effectively implemented.

## Impact of changes

When implementing changes to a business solution, change management steps should be followed to ensure that the project is a success. This includes determining the impact of changes on the value and supply chain.   
Any new ICT project will incur some type of change in process, policy or business. Change management helps in defining these changes, quantifying them and planning for their implementation. Projects that use change management techniques have better chances for success.  
The change management process is a set of steps or activities that a team follow to apply change management to a change in order to drive the transition and ensure the project meets its intended outcomes.

Change can be managed proactively and effectively by: 
- collaboration with staff using the system
- identifying and explaining to all stakeholders the changes that are to be made and the reasons why
- developing the steps for change to happen and documenting these as clearly defined goals and roles
- training and skill development for staff who will be using new systems
- involving relevant staff in each step of the process
- having systems in place to measure success
- making adjustments as and when needed. 

To evaluate the organisational impact of changes you may need to look at conducting a change impact assessment including financial impacts (NPV) or gains (ROI).  

It could cover conducting a What-if Analysis, estimating the effects of change such as who will be affected and what will be affected, any risks or resistance to change and cover both internal or external factors.

Using standard methodologies, standards, procedures and process can support business process change, in particular for introducing changes to the IT processes. Change management can ensure that the impact of changes are minimal.

## Implementing hardware and software solutions

The next stage in developing the business solution is the implementation of the hardware and software solutions so that they can be tested. This may be via a virtual machine, trial software, prototypes or pilot tests.  
Generally speaking, when implementing hardware and software solutions you would need to follow organisational procedures such as workplace health and safety, using a structured methodology or formal documentation.
Testing can include both hardware and software tests. Both can include: Installation, configuration, testing and validation and will have tools, techniques, methodologies, standards processes and procedures that can be followed to ensure testing covers all aspects of functionality, usability, reliability, speed and connectivity.
Most tests will come with a test plan showing the test, expected result, actual result and outcome to action.

- Hardware server testing example: Testing a server

This can include general server stress tests, functionality tests, system stress tests, functionality tests and validation testing.

- Software testing example: Testing a website

This can include:
- Functionality testing
- Usability testing
- Interface testing
- Compatibility testing
- Performance testing
- Security testing.

# Server Testing Overview: Summary for IT Professionals

Windows Server testing via the **Windows Hardware Lab Kit (Windows HLK)** focuses primarily on heavy stress validation, measuring how the system handles client/server I/O, network traffic, CPU utilisation, and memory pressure.

## Key Areas of the Windows Server Test Suite

* **General Server Stress Testing**
* Utilises tools like **LoadGen** to simulate heavy workloads across the system under test (SUT).
* Core stress modules include SQL I/O simulation, local storage I/O, disk stress with verification, client-server storage I/O, and Winsock network traffic.
* Execution workflow requires initial policy setup jobs, long-duration testing (typically 24-hour runs), followed by a policy reset job.


* **Server Virtualisation Validation Program (SVVP)**
* Validates the core operational integrity of virtual machines (VMs).
* Key validation checks cover virtual PCI I/O, virtual SMBIOS, virtual timers, ACPI/PnP functions, and underlying storage capabilities.
* Ensures that virtualization platforms properly expose the virtualised environment state to the running guest OS instance.


* **Certification Requirements Breakdown**
* A full Windows Server certification isn't isolated to just one category; it requires a combination of `System.Client`, `System.Fundamentals`, and `System.Server` test suites.
* HLK Studio automatically detects available hardware features on the target server and maps out the required minimum test sets accordingly.

## **Sample test scenarios for testing a web application – Performance standards and benchmarks**A few other considerations to be included while testing a website are given below:

*   What is the expected load on the server (e.g., number of hits per unit time)?

*   What kind of performance is required under each load condition (such as web server response time, database query response times)?

*   What kind of tools will be required for performance testing (such as web load testing tools, other tools already in house that can be adapted, web robot downloading tools, etc.)?

*   Who is the target audience? What kind of browsers will they be using? What kind of connection speeds will they be using? Are they intra-organizations (thus likely with high connection speeds and similar browsers) or Internet-wide (thus with a wide variety of connection speeds and browser types)?

*   What kind of performance is expected from the client side (e.g., how fast should pages appear, how fast should animations, applets, etc. load and run)?

*   Will the downtime for server and content maintenance/upgrades be allowed? If so, then how much?

*   What kind of security (firewalls, encryption, passwords, etc.) will be required and what is it expected to do? How can it be tested?

*   How reliable is the site’s Internet connections required to be? And how does that affect the backup system or redundant connection requirements and testing?

*   What process will be required to manage updates to the web site’s content?

*   What are the requirements for maintaining, tracking, and controlling page content, graphics, links, etc.?

*   Which HTML specification will be adhered to? How strictly? What variations will be allowed for targeted browsers?

*   Will there be any standard requirements for page appearance and/or graphics throughout a site or parts of a site??

*   How will internal and external links be validated and updated? And how often? will it happen?

*   Can testing be done on the production system, or will a separate test system be required?

*   How are browser caching, variations in browser option settings, dial-up connection variability, and real-world internet ‘traffic congestion’ problems to be accounted for testing?

*   How extensive or customized are the server logging and reporting requirements; are they considered as an integral part of the system and do they require testing?

*   How are CGI programs, applets, JavaScript, ActiveX components, etc. to be maintained, tracked, controlled, and tested?

*   Pages should be 3-5 screens max unless the content is highly focused on a single topic. If larger, provide internal links within the page.

*   The page layouts and design elements should be consistent throughout a site so that it’s clear to the user that they are still on a site.

*   Pages should be as browser-independent as possible, or pages should be provided or generated based on the browser-type.

*   All pages should have links external to the page; there should be no dead-end pages.

*   The page owner, revision date, and a link to a contact person or organization should be included on each page.

## Training needs

Within the change management plan, should be an outline for training or a training plan. This should be developed to ensure that any gaps in knowledge and skills as a result of the business process change are addressed.It is important that staff understand how to use the new processes or changed processes implemented and follow any new policies or procedures.  

*   Training staff formally will encourage and promote policies and procedures so that they are used correctly.

*   Training should be carried out at induction and any future changes to documentation must also be communicated and staff shown how the changes take effect.

*   Training should be a step-by-step process and if needed repeated to consolidate learning.

*   Staff feedback on the procedures and policies is valuable for evaluation.

*   Help documentation or manuals should be provided to assist staff learning.

*   All documentation that is required should be stored in a public area so that all staff can access it.

*   Any skills shortages are addressed.


There may be some procedures that are more complex than others and this may require further training and professional development. A training plan can provide a framework for identifying training needs and gaps in skills and knowledge of personnel.

## Testing, validating and documenting results

A business solution can range from upgrading equipment to design and development of a new website. The testing and validation will be specific to the project.  Any testing and validation will also follow organisational procedures such as completing test plans, using specific validators or recording in specific project management documentation. As an example of the type of testing that may be carried out we will look at the following types of tests that could be performed for a website solution.  

*   Functionality Testing

*   Usability Testing

*   Test for navigation

*   Content checking

*   Interface Testing

*   Compatibility Testing

*   Performance Testing

*   Security Testing.


This type of testing would include testing plans that would provide results on what was expected and the actual outcome.Other type of testing relating to a technical business solution may include:

*   Testing that new procedures work

*   Network performance testing

*   Speed tests

*   User tests.

## validation

Validation is part of testing and an important step to ensuring the technical quality of a solution. 

For example, W3C validation is the process of checking a websites code to determine if it follows the formatting standards. If you fail to validate the pages of your website based on W3C standards, your website will most likely suffer from errors or poor traffic owing to poor formatting and readability.  

## Completion and documentation
Once testing and validation is complete then all the documentation and results should be reviewed for verification by the relevant personnel such as the project manager or client.
Seeking feedback may include making any modifications or changes and then iterating through the testing and validation once again or until it has fully met any testing or business requirement criteria.
Documentation during development should also be kept up to date with any changes or deviations from the original documents.   
This can be done using a standard framework, version control, notes and comments in code, along with messages, emails or progress reports.
The post-development documentation is produced to support the developers of the site and provide manuals and training material for the end users if required (for example how to use the server software).
It can also include testing plans, documentation, user interface changes, end-user documentation, and lesson’s learned reporting.
Once this has been completed then you will be able to obtain the final sign-off on the business solution by the relevant personnel. This could be the client via a meeting and contract or an email from a project manager. For large complex projects you may need to seek sign off and approval from different functional units or specific stakeholders or authorities.
